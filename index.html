Hi, I'm Parth.

I'm a security researcher from India and the founder of ScriptJacker LLP, where I run deep, manual penetration tests on web apps, APIs, mobile apps and networks.

Most of what I find never shows up in a scanner report. An ID you can swap in a request. A checkout that trusts the price your browser sends. A password reset that works for an account that was never created. Bugs like these need someone who reads the app the way its developers did, and then asks what they forgot.

Since November 2023 that habit has earned me 250+ Halls of Fame, 4+ CVEs and a lot of thank you emails from teams at WHO, Boeing, the BBC and the Government of Australia. Right now I'm studying for a BCA in Cybersecurity, mentoring new hunters at Unihackers and leading my CTF team, ScriptJackers.

What I look for

Automated tools are good at known patterns. I spend my time on the bugs that need context: who is allowed to do what, and what happens when you ask twice.

Swap one ID, see someone else's data

It is still one of the most common critical bugs on the internet. I map every object an app exposes, then test who can read, edit or delete it. Invoices, tickets, carts, accounts, files.

From my reportsChanging a numeric bundle ID let me edit another company's service bundle, including its price, from 6,600 to 66,000. Read the writeup

Every role against every endpoint

Can a viewer act like an admin? Can one tenant reach into another? I test each role against each request, not just the buttons the menu happens to show.

What it looks likeVertical escalation to admin actions, horizontal access across users and companies, and hidden endpoints that only check whether you are logged in, not who you are.

Bugs that live in how the business works

Race conditions, coupon stacking, skipped payment steps, prices that come from the client. I learn the real flow first, then look for the step the developers assumed nobody would skip.

From my reportsCheckout flows that accepted a price sent by the browser, letting an order go through at whatever amount the request said.

Getting in without the right key

OTP flows, password resets, OAuth redirects, session handling. The weak spot is usually where two systems hand a user over to each other.

From my reportsStarting a signup, skipping the OTP, then requesting a password reset on a partner subdomain gave me a working account that was never verified or created. Read the writeup

The parts of the app nobody sees

REST and GraphQL. Missing authorization on internal endpoints, mass assignment, broken rate limits and error messages that explain more than they should.

From my reportsTampering with POST parameters made a server return full SQL errors and stack traces, exposing database structure the browser never showed. Read the writeup

Same mindset, newer ground

Prompt injection, jailbreaks, data leakage and tool abuse in chatbots and AI features. The question does not change: where does the system trust input it should not?

What I testSystem prompt exposure, indirect injection through documents and links, cross user data leaks and actions an assistant can be talked into taking.
